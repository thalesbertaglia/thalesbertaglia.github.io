## The practical question

Advertising disclosure rules often sound straightforward: people should be able to recognise commercial content as commercial content. The difficulty begins when that principle has to be measured across platforms, languages, formats, and changing interface conventions.

A disclosure can live in several places:

- inside a caption written by the creator;
- in a platform-provided label;
- visually within an image or video; or
- in context that disappears when a post is collected as data.

That makes the research task less like finding one definitive keyword and more like documenting a chain of imperfect observations. What could a viewer reasonably see? What survives in the available data? Which assumptions were introduced by the collection method?

## A useful failure mode

A classifier may look convincing while learning the vocabulary of a particular dataset rather than the underlying disclosure practice. It can score well and still fail when creators change language, when a platform redesigns its interface, or when the legal question is more specific than the annotation scheme.

The failure is informative. It suggests that evaluation should include time, platform, and jurisdiction—not just a random train/test split.

## What I would test next

The next iteration would compare three kinds of evidence: creator-authored text, platform labels, and a small human assessment of the complete post as it appeared to viewers. The aim would not be to produce a universal compliance score, but to make the limits of each observable signal explicit.

That is also the point of this placeholder: to check whether longer, quieter writing can sit alongside publications, acceptances, revisions, and things that did not work.
